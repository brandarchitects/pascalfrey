Most tech start-ups treat brand like a tin lid. They put it on top once the product is more or less running — logo, palette, a manifesto on the about page. That's not wrong, but it falls short.

Brand is the answer to a simple question: What makes this company unmistakable — and why should anyone care? That question can't be delegated. It's part of the strategy. If you don't answer it, the market will.

## What the AI wave changes

With every model that turns raw text into plausible marketing material, substance becomes more valuable. Not because AI text is bad — it's average-good — but because it's interchangeable. A brand that consists only of phrases is now reproducible in minutes.

What stays is stance. A position someone with experience took, against another possible position. From there follow what the brand says, how it looks, what it promises.

## What doesn't change

The core principles are the same as twenty years ago:

- **Clarity over originality.** Better precise than surprising.
- **One idea per brand.** Not three, not seven.
- **Consistency beats creativity.** A long line drawn straight beats ten brilliant one-offs.

AI as a tool speeds up execution. It doesn't replace the decision that has to be made before.

## What I tell tech start-ups

1. Define early what the brand is not. That list is usually more useful than the positive one.
2. Write the manifesto yourself. Don't delegate, don't "AI-assist" it. You'll have to defend it in front of investors and customers.
3. Invest in a design system, not in one-off hero visuals. The system carries you through five years. The hero visual through one quarterly campaign.
4. Keep the visual vocabulary small. Three sizes, one accent, one image logic. You rarely need more.

Anyone who follows this — AI or no AI — has a brand that lasts.
